The globe

The explorer has a Globe / Map switch. The globe is still empty: a sphere, a 15° grid and an atmosphere glow. Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom. Countries arrive in lesson 05; until then the map stays the default view.

  • The effect is created inside afterNextRender, after the scene exists, so it gets the component's injector explicitly. Outside the constructor, effect() needs to be told which component it belongs to, so it's destroyed with it.

2. Render on demand

A globe is idle most of the time. Drawing 60 frames a second of the same picture wastes battery and warms up laptops. So the loop runs only while something moves:

tick:  controls.update() → render() → nothing moved and no auto-rotation? stop the loop
wake:  pointerdown / pointermove / wheel → start the loop if it's stopped
  • controls.update() returns true while the camera is still changing, during a drag and the damping glide after it.
  • A stopped loop has to be woken by the input itself: OrbitControls only applies movement inside update(), so without a running loop it would never notice a drag.
  • The bug: the first version stopped auto-rotation after a single frame. With damping, OrbitControls ramps auto-rotation up from almost nothing, and the first frame moved less than update()'s "did it move" threshold. Now the loop stays alive explicitly while controls.autoRotate is on.
  • Measured with the draw calls counted: auto-rotating, about 53 frames a second. After a drag, the glide drew 52 frames, then 0 per second while idle.
  • The first drag stops auto-rotation for good: once you're steering, the globe shouldn't move on its own. It's also off with reduced motion (MotionPreference, copied in from learn-angular).
  • An IntersectionObserver stops the loop while the globe is scrolled out of view.
  • ≈ react-three-fiber's <Canvas frameloop="demand"> + invalidate().

3. lon/lat → x, y, z

x = r · cos(lat) · sin(lon)
y = r · sin(lat)            // Three.js is Y-up: the north pole is +Y
z = r · cos(lat) · cos(lon) // lon 0° (Greenwich) faces the camera
  • globe/geo.ts holds lonLatToVector3. Everything on the globe goes through it: the grid now, borders and picking in lessons 05–06, the camera fly-to in lesson 10.
  • The grid is drawn as many 2° segments, so each line follows the curve instead of cutting through the sphere as a straight chord.
  • It sits at radius 1.001, just above the surface. At exactly 1.0 the lines and the sphere would compete for the same depth and flicker (z-fighting).

4. Light and atmosphere

  • The light is attached to the camera, not the scene. Whatever side you look at is lit; a real sun would leave half the data in the dark.
  • The atmosphere is a slightly larger sphere drawn from the inside (BackSide). A small shader makes it brightest at the rim, where the surface faces sideways, and AdditiveBlending adds the glow on top of what's behind it. (Later replaced by a hatched aura when the app moved to a paper-and-ink look; see lesson 05, section 7.)

5. Cost: @defer and cleanup

  • Three.js is big. Imported normally, it pushed the first download from 321 kB to 878 kB, over the 500 kB budget in angular.json.
  • @defer (on immediate) { <app-globe /> } moves the globe and Three.js into their own 563 kB chunk (117 kB gzipped), downloaded the first time the globe is shown. ≈ next/dynamic with ssr: false. Lesson 18 goes further into @defer triggers.
  • Switching to the map destroys the globe. DestroyRef stops the loop, disconnects the observers, removes listeners, disposes every geometry and material, and calls forceContextLoss(). Browsers allow about 16 WebGL contexts per page; a component that leaks them breaks after enough switches.
  • No WebGL (disabled, blocklisted GPU): new WebGLRenderer() throws, the globe emits unsupported, and the explorer switches to the map and disables the Globe button.

6. Later refactor: a scene class and a thin component

After lesson 10, globe.ts had grown to a 310-line constructor: renderer, materials, render loop, fly-to, picking, listeners, five effects and cleanup, all sharing local variables, with lessons 09, 12 and 15 still to land there. It was split in two:

globe/globe-scene.ts: GlobeSceneglobe/globe.ts: Globe
A plain class, no Angular. Renderer, camera, controls, materials, the render loop, picking, flights, dispose().The Angular side: inputs and models, the tooltip, one effect per input that calls a scene method.
Methods in: setFeatures, setBorders, setHighlight, setAutoRotate, flyTo. Callbacks out: hover(id, x, y), select(id).Turns the callbacks into signal writes, plus a direct style.translate for the tooltip.
  • ≈ In React: a component holding a ref to an imperative object, with a useEffect per prop that calls a method on it. The scene can be unit-tested without Angular.
  • invalidate(): changes (new data, a hover, a selection) no longer call render() themselves. They mark the scene dirty and wake the loop, and the next tick draws once. A tick renders only if the camera moved, something was invalidated, or auto-rotation is on. Measured: three hover changes in a row → 1 render; idle after a flight → 0. Resizing still renders immediately, since a resized canvas is cleared and would flash blank.
  • A cache across mounts: switching Map → Globe creates a new globe, which used to rasterize the ID map again (~90 ms) and re-densify the borders. Both depend only on the data, so they're cached in a module-level WeakMap keyed by the data arrays. Measured: three switches, one rasterization. The textures are still per globe, because each globe has its own WebGL context.
  • Behaviour is unchanged: the same flights, picking, hover, render on demand and cleanup, checked in the browser after the split.

7. Later: smooth zoom, natural drag

  • The problem: OrbitControls' damping smooths rotation, not zoom. Each mouse-wheel notch moved the camera in one jump, so zooming felt jaggy.
  • The fix: the wheel no longer moves the camera; it moves a target distance. Every frame the camera covers a fixed fraction of what's left (easeZoom in globe-scene.ts), so a notch becomes a 0.3 s glide. Measured for one notch: 2.9 → 2.83 → 2.73 → 2.69 … → 2.5.
  • Multiplicative steps: a notch multiplies the target by e^(px · 0.0015), about ×1.16, so it feels the same close up and far away. Scrolling again mid-glide continues from the target, so fast scrolling adds up instead of stalling. The target is clamped to the zoom limits (1.8–6).
  • Frame-rate independent: the fraction is 1 − e^(−dt / 90 ms), using the time since the last frame, so the glide takes the same time at 60 Hz and 144 Hz. A plain "move 15% per frame" would zoom twice as fast on a 120 Hz screen.
  • Taking the wheel from OrbitControls: the listener is registered in the capture phase. On the canvas itself, capture listeners run before OrbitControls' own wheel listener, and stopImmediatePropagation() keeps it from also jumping. Touch pinch is still OrbitControls' job. The listener isn't passive, so preventDefault() can stop ctrl+wheel (a trackpad pinch) from zooming the whole page.
  • Wheel deltas come in pixels, lines (Firefox mice) or pages; all three are converted to pixels first.
  • Drag speed follows the zoom ("grab and follow"): a fixed rotateSpeed (it was 0.5) turns the globe the same angle per pixel at every zoom, far too much up close. OrbitControls turns by 2π · rotateSpeed per screen height of drag, and one pixel covers 2 · tan(fov/2) · (distance − 1) / height of the surface facing the camera. Setting them equal gives rotateSpeed = tan(fov/2) · (distance − 1) / π: 0.22 at the start, 0.09 fully zoomed in, 0.58 zoomed out. It's updated every frame, and DRAG_FEEL scales it if you want more or less.
  • Measured by projecting the grabbed point back to the screen after a drag: a vertical 80 px drag moved it 78 px (72 px zoomed in). A horizontal 120 px drag moved it about 100 px. That's not an error: dragging sideways turns the globe around its north–south axis, and away from the equator a point travels a smaller circle (cos 32° ≈ 0.85 at the start view's latitude), which is how a real globe behaves.
  • The tooltip on drag: while a button is held, hover isn't updated, so the tooltip used to hang where it was while the globe turned under it. Now, the moment a press moves past the 5 px click tolerance, the hover is cleared once (tooltip and highlight). A plain click keeps them; they come back with the first move after the drag.

Review checklist

  • Switch to Globe. In DevTools → Network, a globe chunk loads only now, not on page load.
  • It auto-rotates. Drag once: it glides to a stop and stays still. Zoom in and out: it stops at the limits.
  • DevTools → Performance, record 3 seconds of an idle globe after a drag: no frames are drawn.
  • Switch Globe → Map → Globe a few times: no console warnings about too many WebGL contexts.
  • Turn on reduced motion in your OS: the globe doesn't auto-rotate.
